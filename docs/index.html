Choosing an AI model for ad creative

A way to pick an image or video model that still works after the next release.

I read 874 lines naming an AI video model across 114 threads from marketers and creators. For every line asking which model should I use? there were thirty-three announcing a new one. Almost everything written about models is release news. Almost none of it helps you decide.

The questions people do ask mostly go unanswered:

Which is the best AI video generation model?
Seedance 2.5 vs Seedance 2.0 for AI UGC — who got it better?

"Which is best" has no stable answer, because the field moves faster than any answer lasts. One model line went through several versions inside a single month of these threads. So this guide doesn't rank models. It gives you the axes that matter and a test you can re-run.

The four axes that actually differ

AxisWhat it meansMatters for ads?
Fidelity per frameHow good a single still looksLeast. Nearly every current model clears the bar, and it's all launch reels show you
Consistency across a setSame face, product and room across forty clipsMost. It decides whether you can run a campaign
Physics under motionFast movement, hands, objects interactingSituational. Vital for product demos, irrelevant for talking heads
Cost per usable outputPrice divided by the share you'd actually runMost. A cheaper model with twice the discard rate isn't cheaper

For ad work, choose on consistency and cost per usable output. That's the opposite of how launch posts are ordered.

The switching cost nobody mentions

If your ads depend on a recurring face, the model isn't just a tool. Your ads depend on it. Switch models and the face changes, and any recognition you'd built resets. That recognition is the only part of this that compounds.

  • Stay current: always use the newest model and accept that the look drifts. Right for volume testing, where every ad stands alone.
  • Pin and hold: lock one model for the life of a persona. Right for any face people are meant to recognise.

Most teams are in the second position but behave as if they're in the first, upgrading on release day.

A test that survives the next release

  1. Write down which axis limits you, in one sentence.
  2. Keep three fixed prompts in a file: one talking to camera, one product interaction, one that must match a face from an earlier clip.
  3. Generate ten of each per model and count the ones you'd actually run. That discard rate is the number that maps to money.
  4. Decide your posture (current or pinned) before you test, because it changes which result matters.
  5. Re-run the test when something ships. Don't switch on the announcement.
